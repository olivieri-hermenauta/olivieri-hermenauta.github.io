# Molecules as Sentences: Medicine in the Age of AI

*From explanation to performance*

<img src="/img/protein-sentence.png" alt="Microscopia" style="max-width:60%; margin: 2rem auto; display: block;">

In 2019, researchers at Insilico Medicine turned an artificial intelligence platform toward a disease with no known cure: idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, which progressively destroys lung tissue.
In less than eighteen months—an extraordinarily short timeframe for a process that usually takes four to six years—the AI identified a new molecular target and generated a drug candidate at a cost of under $2.6 million.

The compound, named rentosertib, advanced to phase 2a clinical trials. Preliminary results, published in Nature Medicine in 2025, showed not only safety and good tolerability, but also measurable improvement in lung function—something existing treatments rarely achieve.

What makes this case especially interesting is not just the speed, but what happened within the process itself.

## What AI is actually doing
The AI that generated rentosertib does not understand what a disease is. It has no theory of pulmonary fibrosis and does not formulate hypotheses in the classical human sense.

It operates over vast molecular patterns, testing combinations and evaluating probabilities of success across spaces of possibility that no human laboratory could explore in useful time.

Instead of seeking explanations, it explores possibilities.
And within that space, it finds solutions.

## Molecules as sentences
Perhaps the best way to understand this phenomenon is through an analogy with language.
Just as a well-formed sentence follows syntactic rules, a functional molecule follows structural rules of chemistry. In both cases, validity precedes full understanding.

AI navigates this universe like a writer: generating structures, testing variations, adjusting connections, and evaluating outcomes.

A molecule created by AI is, in this sense, a chemical sentence—syntactically correct before it is fully understood. And, as in language, this structural correctness can produce real effects in the world before we know exactly why.
Not entirely new.

This is not entirely new in the history of medicine.

Aspirin was used for decades before its mechanism of action was understood. Penicillin was discovered by accident.

What changes now is scale and systematization. AI does not occasionally stumble upon solutions it does not understand—it turns this inversion into a method.

## The paradox
A profound shift emerges.

For centuries, science operated under the assumption that understanding should precede intervention: first explain, then apply.

Artificial intelligence reverses this order—systematically, not accidentally.

We have never understood so little about underlying mechanisms—and never produced so many therapeutic effects.

AI does not know what a bacterium is, yet it can help kill one. It does not understand pulmonary fibrosis, yet it can generate a molecule that acts against it.
This is not just a technical advance. It is a change in epistemological regime.

## From understanding to performance
We are witnessing a transition from a science centered on explanation to one oriented toward performance.

The goal is no longer necessarily to know what a disease is in its essence, but to discover what works against it.

In this new context, scientific practice begins to resemble what Ludwig Wittgenstein called “language games”: systems in which meaning emerges from use, rather than from a fully accessible underlying essence.

An effective molecule does not need to be fully understood from the outset. It only needs to work.

Truth, here, becomes operative.

## A science without a subject?
There is something unsettling in this inversion.

Unlike historical discoveries made by chance, there is no illusion of subject here. We do not project intention or consciousness onto the machine.

What remains is pure efficacy—a molecule acting in the physical world independently of any understanding, human or artificial.

As we delegate not only calculation but discovery itself to machines, an uncomfortable question arises:

What are we doing, exactly, when we cure without fully understanding?

If medicine can, in part, be “written” as language—if molecules can be generated as sentences within a formal space we explore without fully mastering—then the challenge is no longer only to cure disease.

It is to interpret what it means to cure when understanding is no longer the starting point.
