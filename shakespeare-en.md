What’s in a Name?

There is a question that has crossed the centuries with deceptive simplicity:

What’s in a name? That which we call a rose
By any other name would smell as sweet.

The line is spoken by Juliet in Romeo and Juliet. The context is well known: impossible love, names that divide, families that turn words into destinies. For Juliet, the name “Montague” is an accident — an external label. Romeo would remain Romeo — that is, what he is — even if he were called something else.

The rose would still have the same scent.

For a long time, this intuition seemed almost self-evident. Names designate; they do not create. Language points to the world but does not constitute it. Between word and thing there is a distance — sometimes small, sometimes vast, but real.

Today, we are no longer so sure.

Across different spheres of public life, there is a growing conviction that changing vocabulary is a way of intervening in reality itself. Terms are abandoned, replaced, reformulated. This is not merely a matter of semantic precision, but of normative correction. Some words come to be seen as inadequate not because they are false, but because they produce undesirable effects.

Vocabulary becomes a field of dispute.

It is no coincidence that this debate has taken on a concrete form in recent days. xAI, the company founded by Elon Musk, has filed a lawsuit challenging Colorado’s new AI anti-discrimination law, arguing that regulating the language produced by artificial intelligence systems oversteps the bounds of free speech. The case is local, but the question is general.

The motivation is not hard to understand. Language shapes perception. It emphasizes, conceals, associates. Naming is not a neutral act. Throughout history, words have been used to reduce, stigmatize, simplify the other. Correcting this seems, at first glance, like progress.

But there is a subtle — and decisive — line between recognizing the power of language and attributing to it an absolute power.

When we begin to act as if names could alter essence, something shifts. Language ceases to be mediation and becomes an instrument for administering reality. We no longer describe the world; we attempt to adjust it through words.

The rose begins to lose its scent — or at least we begin to act as if that scent depended on its name.

This movement does not occur in a vacuum. It coincides with a broader transformation: the centrality of language as environment. We do not merely speak and write — we inhabit systems that continuously produce language: social networks, search engines, digital assistants, artificial intelligence models.

These systems are not neutral with respect to vocabulary. They are trained, tuned, filtered. They incorporate implicit norms about what should be said and how it should be said. What would once have been a matter of open debate becomes a technical parameter.

Artificial intelligence did not invent this tendency. It makes it operational at scale.

And this is where Shakespeare’s intuition returns to trouble us.

If the rose remains the same regardless of its name, then there is something in reality that resists language — something that cannot be reconfigured by decree. But if our experience of the world is mediated by language — and today it is more than ever — then changing names does, in fact, alter how that world appears.

Between these two positions, a tension opens that cannot be easily resolved.

This impasse is not new. It already appears, in a strikingly unsettling way, in Plato’s Cratylus.

There, the question is whether names are conventional — mere human agreements — or whether they bear some natural relation to the things they designate. The reader expects a solution. The dialogue does not provide one. It opens the problem instead.

Neither view is sufficient on its own. Names are not entirely arbitrary; nor do they fully capture the essence of what they name.

From its philosophical beginnings, language reveals itself as an unstable medium — at once necessary and insufficient.
What now reappears as a political dispute was already inscribed, from the start, as a philosophical problem.

On one side, Juliet’s confidence: being does not depend on the name.

On the other, contemporary practice: the name shapes the field of the visible, the acceptable, the sayable.

Perhaps Shakespeare’s question has not lost its relevance. Perhaps it has become more urgent.

What’s in a name?

If the answer is “nothing,” we risk ignoring the real power of language.

If the answer is “everything,” we risk reducing the world to the vocabulary we choose to impose.

Between these two extremes, the problem remains — and with it, the task.

To name the world without pretending to replace it.

Naming frames.
