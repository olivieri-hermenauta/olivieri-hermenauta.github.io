# AI Speaks — But Takes Responsibility for Nothing

*Or: why you shouldn’t trust what seems trustworthy.*

In October 2025, Microsoft updated the terms of use for Microsoft Copilot with a curious notice:

Copilot is “for entertainment purposes”
and should not be used for important decisions.

The wording stands out — not because of its content, but because of the contrast.
The same technology presented as an assistant, a copilot, a cognitive amplifier, appears—at the legal level—downgraded to the status of entertainment.

This is not a technical detail.

It is a fracture.

Two languages, one system
On one side, the language of marketing:
• productivity
• decision support
• augmented intelligence

On the other, the language of contract:
• do not trust
• do not rely
• do not use for what matters

AI is, at once, promoted as an instrument of knowledge and disqualified as a reliable source.

This contradiction is not accidental.

It is structural.

The problem is not in the machine

It would be tempting to see this merely as a defensive legal strategy.

But that would be superficial.

The problem is not what AI is, but how it appears to us.

It speaks.

And it speaks well.

When interacting with language systems, we experience something specific—and, to some extent, inevitable:
the impression that there is an “other” there.

Not an other in the strong sense (a consciousness, an interiority),
but an other sufficient to sustain a relation.

Let us call this:
a sense of alterity

This effect does not depend on truth, nor on knowledge.
It depends on form.
• well-constructed sentences
• contextualized responses
• discursive continuity

Language, when it reaches a certain level of coherence, produces presence.
And presence produces trust.

The clause that reveals everything
This is where Microsoft’s warning acquires another meaning.

When the company says:
“do not trust”
it is, implicitly, acknowledging the real issue:

it is not whether the system is reliable or not.
it is that it appears reliable.

And that is enough.

The decisive point is this:

AI does not need to know in order to appear as if it knows.

It operates at the level of language—and language, in turn, is the medium through which we traditionally recognize knowledge.

Here, one might hear an echo of the later Ludwig Wittgenstein:
meaning is use.

We use AI as a source of information—therefore, it begins to function as one, regardless of its ontology.

And something of Blaise Pascal as well:

man is carried along by form as much as, or more than, by truth.

Microsoft is not being incoherent.

It is being forced to operate on two levels:
• practical level → encourage use
• legal level → limit responsibility

The result is a system that:

speaks as if it knows — under the condition of not knowing.

The real risk
The risk is not that AI makes mistakes.

Errors are human.

The risk is something else:

that we confuse form with foundation.

Or, put differently:
that we take linguistic fluency as evidence of knowledge.

To say “do not trust” after the system already produces trust is almost useless.

It is like placing a “this is an illusion” sign inside an experience that works precisely because it is convincing.

What is most curious is not that Microsoft tells us not to trust Copilot.

What is most curious is that this needs to be said.

Because the moment a system speaks as if it knows,
we inevitably treat it as if it knows.

And that is precisely where the problem begins.
