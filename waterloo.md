#  Waterloo

*What cannot be seen when history happens*

He rides without quite knowing where to.

The name of the battle already exists — Waterloo — but not for him. For Fabrice del Dongo, there is only movement: officers passing by, orders that do not quite reach him, shots that seem to come from everywhere and nowhere at once.

In The Charterhouse of Parma, Stendhal does something rare: he removes from war what will later be attributed to it — clarity.

There is no overview. No map. Not even the certainty of being in the right place.

Above all, there is no clear line between friend and enemy.

Fabrice sees soldiers, horses, smoke. He sees courage and panic intertwined. But he does not see the enemy as a distinct figure. It does not present itself as such. There is no fixed face, no stable form. There are only fleeting encounters, brief collisions, noise.

And yet, it is here that the fate of Europe is decided.

The distance between the event and its interpretation could not be greater.

Because somewhere else — higher up, further away — the battle must make sense. It must be named, described, organized. It must produce an outcome. It must, above all, distinguish sides.

It is on this other plane that the figure of Napoleon Bonaparte emerges.

Not the man crossing the mud, but the one who sees — or must see — the field as a whole. To him is attributed the capacity to order chaos, to identify the enemy, to turn the confused flow of events into decision.

Today, this same ambition reappears in another form. When companies such as Palantir Technologies argue for the use of advanced systems to map threats, anticipate conflicts, and render the enemy visible even before confrontation, the promise remains unchanged: to reduce uncertainty, to stabilize the field, to make legible what, on the ground, is not.

But Fabrice’s scene remains.

It endures as a kind of fissure: a reminder that what is decided as clear may, at the point where it happens, be irreducibly opaque.

Perhaps every war begins like this.

Not with the identification of the enemy, but with its absence.

Or rather: with the impossibility of recognizing it in any stable way.

The enemy, then, is not only the one against whom one fights. It is also what must be constructed for the fight to make sense.

Between the field and the map, something is lost — and something is invented.

Fabrice crosses Waterloo without understanding it. Later, others will understand it for him.

They will give names, draw lines, fix positions.

They will say where the enemy was.

But this clarity comes after.

At the very moment history unfolds, what there is instead is something else: a succession of incomplete perceptions, where seeing is not yet understanding, and where the enemy does not present itself as evidence.

Perhaps this is what remains, despite everything.

Not the battle as system, but as experience.

Not the enemy as given, but as problem.
