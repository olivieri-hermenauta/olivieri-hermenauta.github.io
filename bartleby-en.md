# Bartleby and the language without commitment

*It is possible to operate within language without fully assuming what one does.*

Some texts stay with us not for what they say, but for the kind of unease they produce.

Bartleby, the Scrivener, by Herman Melville, is one of them.

The story is simple. A lawyer hires a copyist — a worker of language — to reproduce documents with precision. For a while, Bartleby performs well. Quiet, efficient, almost invisible.

Then, when asked to perform a trivial task, he replies:
I would prefer not to.

The sentence returns. With slight variations, but always the same structure. Bartleby does not argue, protest, or rebel. He simply suspends the act.

And in doing so, he disorganizes everything around him.

Bartleby is a worker of language. His role is to reproduce texts. Not to create, interpret, or debate — only to copy.

Yet his refusal is not exactly a refusal of work. It is something more subtle: a refusal to fully adhere to what he does.

He remains present. In a sense, still available. But something breaks between the action and the commitment to the action.

Language remains. The bond with meaning weakens.

That is what makes the text so unsettling.

There is no dramatic conflict. No psychological explanation. No redemption.

Only a presence that operates within language — and at the same time withdraws from it.

Reading Bartleby today produces a curious effect.

We are used to thinking of intellectual work as a space of engagement: to understand, to elaborate, to respond. Language would be the place where thought organizes itself and becomes accountable.

But Bartleby suggests another possibility.

It is possible to operate within language without fully assuming what one does.

In his case, through refusal.

In ours, perhaps, through the opposite path.

We have never produced so much language. Never written so much, so quickly, with such fluency.

But this fluency may conceal a growing distance between what is said and who is responsible for it.

Bartleby said: I would prefer not to.

We may be approaching a more ambiguous condition — one in which refusal is no longer necessary.

Language keeps flowing.

And yet, something in the order of commitment may begin to dissipate.

Bartleby stands still, facing a wall.

It is a simple image, almost trivial.

But it may say more than it seems.

Between the silence of one who refuses and the fluency of one who no longer needs to decide, there is a subtle — and decisive — shift in the relation between language and responsibility.

And it is possible that we have not yet fully grasped the extent of that shift.
