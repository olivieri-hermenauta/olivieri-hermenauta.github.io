# Digital Regulation and the Architecture of Freedom

*What Luciano Floridi’s philosophy can teach us about the risks of digital overregulation.*

<img src="/img/lei-felca.png" alt="Exigência documentos" style="max-width:100%; margin: 2rem auto; display: block;">

Recent regulatory initiatives in Australia have reignited a global debate about digital governance. Measures aimed at protecting minors online—particularly age-verification requirements—are raising concerns among technologists and civil-liberties advocates. The worry is not the goal itself, which few would dispute, but the way such regulations might reshape the technical architecture of the internet.

When legal frameworks designed for large digital platforms begin to interact with the deeper layers of digital infrastructure, unintended consequences can arise. In some cases, critics argue, regulatory requirements may even end up constraining open technological ecosystems such as free and open-source software.

These tensions are not unique to Australia. Around the world, governments are attempting to regulate powerful digital technologies while struggling to keep pace with their technical complexity.
To understand the deeper philosophical stakes of this moment, it is worth turning to one of the most influential voices in contemporary digital ethics: the Italian philosopher Luciano Floridi.

Floridi is widely known for his work in the philosophy of information, a field that seeks to understand how digital technologies are transforming the environment in which human life unfolds. According to Floridi, we now inhabit what he calls the infosphere—a hybrid space in which human beings, algorithms, and technical systems continuously interact.

From this perspective, many popular fears about artificial intelligence appear misplaced. The idea that machines will suddenly become conscious and dominate humanity belongs largely to the realm of science fiction.

Artificial intelligence, Floridi argues, is better understood as a reservoir of agency: systems capable of performing tasks and making operational decisions at a scale previously unimaginable. As he sometimes notes with a touch of irony, AI possesses something closer to “the intelligence of a refrigerator” than to human cognition—yet its operational power can be extraordinary.

For Floridi, the real risks of digital technology lie elsewhere: in the opacity of large technological infrastructures, the manipulation of public opinion, the environmental costs of computational systems, and—perhaps most importantly—the problem of technological governance.

This is where regulatory models begin to diverge.

In the United States, digital governance has traditionally emphasized innovation and market dynamics. In Europe, by contrast, regulators have increasingly framed digital policy around the protection of fundamental rights.
Floridi has often expressed cautious optimism about this European approach. One of its most discussed consequences is the so-called “Brussels Effect”, a phenomenon in which European regulations gradually influence global technological standards.
Yet regulatory ambition always carries risks of its own.

When policymakers attempt to regulate technologies whose technical foundations they only partially understand, well-intentioned laws can sometimes produce unexpected distortions. Rules designed with large corporate platforms in mind may inadvertently affect decentralized technological communities that operate under entirely different structures.

Open-source ecosystems illustrate this problem clearly. Projects such as Linux, for example, are not controlled by a single corporation but developed by distributed communities of contributors around the world. Regulatory frameworks that assume centralized control can therefore struggle to accommodate such decentralized environments.

Floridi himself acknowledges that digital transformation is unfolding at a pace that outstrips the adaptive capacity of traditional institutions. In discussions about the future of work, for instance, he has suggested that societies may need to “borrow from the future” to support social stability during technological transitions.

The idea is that part of the wealth generated by automation and artificial intelligence could be redistributed in order to cushion the disruptive effects of rapid technological change.

Yet this solution also raises legitimate concerns. Expanding systems of technological governance and social protection may protect citizens — but they may also expand the scope of centralized power.

The challenge, therefore, is not whether digital technologies should be regulated. That question has already been settled by the sheer scale of their social impact.

The real question is whether societies can develop forms of regulation that protect citizens without undermining the open technological infrastructures that made the digital world possible in the first place.
Artificial intelligence will undoubtedly expand the capabilities of modern societies. But the way it is governed will determine whether that power becomes widely distributed—or increasingly concentrated.

In the end, the difference between technologies that empower citizens and technologies that monitor them will not be decided by algorithms.

It will be decided by politics.
