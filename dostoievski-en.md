# The Trial of Dmitri Karamazov, Today

*Security, language, and the impasse of deciding*

There are trials in which the problem is not the lack of evidence, but its excess. Every detail seems to fit somewhere, every testimony aligns — and yet the whole refuses to converge. Truth does not disappear; it multiplies.

This is what unsettles us in Dmitri’s trial in The Brothers Karamazov. It is not a matter of ignorance or obvious error. On the contrary: the facts are all there, reiterated, examined, rearranged. What changes is the way they are narrated. Each discourse recomposes reality according to its own logic, and the result is a proliferation of coherent yet incompatible versions.

The court does not discover the truth. It produces it — or, more precisely, it produces a decision among possible truths.

In Fyodor Dostoevsky, this tension is still resolved. The jury decides. The sentence is pronounced. It may be unjust, it may be flawed, but it exists. In the end, there is a gesture that closes the process, even if it does not eliminate the ambiguity running through it.

Today, the situation is somewhat different.

The terms have changed — people speak of security, alignment, control, risk. Institutions such as the White House and companies such as Anthropic occupy the place once held by more easily identifiable figures. Yet the mechanism, at first glance, seems the same: there is a problem, there are arguments, and there is a need to decide.

And still, something escapes.

It is not merely that positions diverge. The words themselves no longer coincide with themselves. “Security” does not designate the same thing in every discourse. “Risk” changes its nature according to context. “Control” may mean limitation, governance, or the illusion of mastery.

The disagreement lies not only in the conclusions, but in the vocabulary that makes those conclusions possible.

The courtroom is full.

No one doubts the gravity of the case. There are reports, projections, technical assessments. There are graphs anticipating scenarios and testimonies describing systems whose operation can no longer be reduced to a single intention.

The representative of the State speaks first. His voice is firm, though measured. He insists on the need to preserve order, prevent irreversible harm, and ensure that decisions with collective consequences do not escape the public sphere. He uses the word “security” frequently.

The engineer replies. He does not deny the risks, but qualifies them. He explains architectures, mitigation mechanisms, layers of oversight. For him, “security” is an emergent property: adjustable, provisional, always in process.

A third instance is then called to testify. In the records, it is described as a large language model, commonly known as artificial intelligence. Its responses are precise, but it never takes a position. It reformulates questions, returns statements, clarifies ambiguities — without ever fixing a final meaning.

The prosecutor intervenes. He demands clear definitions.

“What, after all, do we mean by security?”

The representative of the State answers first. Security is the absence of relevant threats to society as a whole.

The engineer adjusts the definition. Security is the continuous minimization of risks within acceptable parameters.

The third voice adds: security is a term whose application depends on the context in which it is evaluated.

The prosecutor insists. He asks for a single, operational definition.

The answers begin to overlap. One definition refers to another, which corrects it, expands it, renders it dependent on a prior condition. The sentences become longer, more precise — and less conclusive.

The judge watches.

For a while, it seems that the proceedings advance. Each side speaks clearly, presents consistent reasons, answers objections rigorously. There is no confusion, no evident mistake. Everything functions as it should.

Except for the fact that nothing settles.

The judge then asks the final question:

“Who decides?”

A brief pause follows.

The representative of the State speaks of guidelines, supervisory bodies, institutional responsibility.

The engineer mentions protocols, governance models, iterative processes.

The third voice responds:

“The decision results from the interaction of multiple factors.”

The judge reformulates the question:

“Who signs?”

No direct answer comes.

Someone mentions a committee. Another speaks of the system. A third suggests that the very formulation of the question may be inadequate.

The silence that follows is not one of hesitation, but of saturation. Everything has already been said — and yet nothing permits closure.

The judge consults his notes. The words are all there: security, risk, control, decision. But they refuse to align themselves into a sentence that would not itself become merely another version among others.

He hesitates for a moment, as if it were still possible to find the exact term, the formulation that would resolve the impasse.

He does not find it.

And so the session was adjourned for lack of terms.
